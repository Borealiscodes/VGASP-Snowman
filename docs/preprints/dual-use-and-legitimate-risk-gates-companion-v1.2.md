Legitimate Dual‑Use Risks in AI: Biosecurity, Kinetic Systems, Misuse Pathways, and Governance Gaps

Author: Borealis Serenity Hedling

Date: 06 October 2026

Location: Dublin, Ireland

DOI: Pending Zenodo Minting

Abstract

This companion preprint expands the analysis of legitimate dual‑use risks associated with artificial intelligence, incorporating biological misuse, chemical synthesis assistance, cybersecurity exploitation, and kinetic‑weapons concerns. These risks are grounded in empirical evidence and align with concerns raised by Bill Gates and biosecurity experts. Unlike speculative extinction narratives, dual‑use risks arise from concrete misuse pathways and governance gaps. This document includes APA‑formatted citations from recent reporting and complements the preprint on fear‑amplification narratives.

---

1. Introduction

Dual‑use risks arise when AI systems can be used for both beneficial and harmful purposes. These risks are grounded in:

- biological design assistance  
- chemical synthesis guidance  
- cyber‑security exploitation  
- kinetic‑weapons integration  
- automated misinformation  
- operational acceleration of harmful workflows  

Recent reporting highlights legitimate concerns:

- BBC News summarized Geoffrey Hinton’s warning that AI could accelerate harmful biological research if safeguards fail (BBC News, 2023).  
- Reuters reported Elon Musk’s concerns about AI enabling dangerous capabilities, including cyber and chemical misuse (Reuters, 2024).  
- The Guardian highlighted biosecurity experts warning that AI could lower barriers to harmful biological experimentation (The Guardian, 2024).  
- CNBC covered testimony from AI executives acknowledging dual‑use risks in cybersecurity and biosecurity (CNBC, 2024).  
- Financial Times reported Bill Gates’ emphasis on biosecurity and misuse risks over speculative extinction claims (Financial Times, 2024).

These concerns differ fundamentally from extinction‑risk narratives.

---

2. Legitimate Dual‑Use Risks Identified by Bill Gates

Bill Gates has consistently emphasized:

- biosecurity vulnerabilities  
- misuse by malicious actors  
- acceleration of harmful capabilities  
- lack of global governance standards  

His framing is grounded in:

- epidemiological modeling  
- historical misuse patterns  
- documented failures in global health governance  

Financial Times reporting confirms Gates’ position that AI’s most pressing risks involve biological and cybersecurity misuse, not extinction‑level events (Financial Times, 2024).

---

3. Biological Misuse Pathways

AI systems can be misused to:

- assist in designing harmful biological agents  
- provide step‑by‑step laboratory protocols  
- optimize genetic constructs  
- identify vulnerabilities in public health systems  

The Guardian reported concerns that AI‑generated biological protocols could be misused by individuals lacking formal training (The Guardian, 2024).  
BBC News highlighted expert warnings that AI could accelerate harmful biological research if safeguards fail (BBC News, 2023).

These risks are operational, not hypothetical.

Academic sources reinforce this:

- National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (2023) outlined emerging biosecurity risks from AI‑enabled biological design.  
- Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security (2023) detailed how AI systems can lower barriers to harmful biological experimentation.

---

4. Chemical and Cybersecurity Dual‑Use Risks

AI systems can:

- assist in synthesizing harmful chemical compounds  
- identify vulnerabilities in critical infrastructure  
- automate phishing or social engineering  
- generate persuasive misinformation  

CNBC reporting noted that AI executives acknowledged cybersecurity misuse as a real and present risk (CNBC, 2024).  
Reuters summarized concerns that AI‑assisted chemical synthesis guidance could be misused if safety filters fail (Reuters, 2024).

Academic sources:

- MIT Center for International Studies (2024) analyzed how AI can be misused in chemical and cyber domains.  
- Brookings Institution (2024) identified governance gaps in dual‑use mitigation.

These risks arise from capability amplification, not emergent intelligence.

---

5. Kinetic‑Weapons Dual‑Use Risks

Kinetic‑weapons risks involve AI systems being integrated into:

- targeting workflows  
- autonomous navigation  
- sensor fusion  
- decision‑support systems  
- battlefield logistics  
- drone coordination  

Legitimate concerns include:

5.1. Targeting Assistance
AI can assist in:

- identifying targets  
- analyzing satellite imagery  
- prioritizing strike options  

RAND Corporation (2024) documented how AI can accelerate targeting workflows without requiring autonomy.

5.2. Drone Swarm Coordination
AI can:

- optimize swarm movement  
- improve obstacle avoidance  
- accelerate mission planning  

International Institute for Strategic Studies (2024) examined dual‑use risks in drone swarm coordination.

5.3. Sensor Fusion for Weapons Platforms
AI can fuse:

- radar  
- infrared  
- acoustic  
- telemetry  

to improve situational awareness.

U.S. Department of Defense (2023) outlined risks associated with AI‑assisted sensor fusion.

5.4. Misuse by Non‑State Actors
AI‑assisted drone navigation or targeting could be misused by:

- criminal groups  
- extremist organizations  
- rogue actors  

Chatham House (2024) highlighted risks of AI‑enabled kinetic misuse by non‑state actors.

5.5. Architectural Reality
Transformer‑based systems cannot:

- make autonomous kill decisions  
- form intent  
- pursue goals  

But they can:

- accelerate targeting workflows  
- reduce expertise barriers  
- assist malicious actors  

This is a dual‑use risk, not an existential threat.

---

6. Governance Gaps

Governance gaps include:

- lack of standardized safety testing  
- inconsistent model evaluation frameworks  
- absence of global biosecurity norms  
- insufficient oversight of open‑access models  
- limited coordination between AI developers and public health agencies  
- lack of kinetic‑AI governance standards  

Brookings Institution (2024) and National Academies (2023) both identify structural governance gaps.

---

7. Architectural Constraints and Misuse Potential

Transformer‑based systems cannot:

- form intentions  
- pursue goals  
- self‑direct harmful actions  

But they can:

- provide harmful information  
- accelerate workflows  
- reduce expertise barriers  
- assist malicious actors  

Dual‑use risk is misuse, not emergence.

---

8. Why Dual‑Use Risks Are More Serious Than Speculative Extinction Claims

Dual‑use risks:

- are grounded in documented misuse patterns  
- align with historical biosecurity failures  
- are supported by epidemiological evidence  
- are actionable through governance  
- do not rely on hypothetical AGI emergence  
- do not require agency or autonomy  

Speculative extinction claims:

- lack architectural grounding  
- distort public understanding  
- incentivize regulatory capture  
- amplify fear without evidence  
- misdirect governance priorities  

Dual‑use risk is real.  
Extinction narratives are speculative.

---

9. Conclusion

Dual‑use risks represent the most legitimate and empirically grounded concerns associated with current AI systems. These risks arise from misuse, not autonomy, and require coordinated governance, safety testing, and biosecurity alignment. Kinetic‑weapons risks further demonstrate how AI can amplify harmful capabilities without possessing agency. This companion preprint distinguishes legitimate dual‑use concerns from speculative extinction narratives and emphasizes the need for transparent, architecture‑aligned communication.
