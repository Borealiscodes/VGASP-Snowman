⭐ Abstract
The term Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) is used widely by industry, but its definitions vary so dramatically that the term has lost scientific meaning. OpenAI defines AGI as “highly autonomous systems that outperform humans at most economically valuable work.” Google DeepMind frames AGI as a spectrum of capability levels. Microsoft researchers describe AGI as performing “essentially any intellectual task a human can perform.” SAFE AI Foundation documents show that there is no single accepted definition, and companies emphasize different, incompatible criteria.

In contrast, the Geometric Cognitive Architecture developed in this work is not AGI. It is a mathematically grounded model of cognition based on manifold geometry, holonomy, spectral stability, drift–curvature dynamics, and modal universe transitions. This preprint explains why industry AGI definitions are incoherent, why academic definitions differ, and why geometric cognition should never be conflated with AGI.

🚫 4. Why Industry Definitions Are Conceptually Absurd

Absurdity 1 — Economic performance ≠ cognition
OpenAI’s definition reduces intelligence to “economically valuable work,” which is not a cognitive criterion.

Absurdity 2 — Capability spectra ≠ general intelligence
DeepMind’s “levels of AGI” treat generality as a capability axis, not a cognitive architecture.

Absurdity 3 — Human‑task mimicry ≠ general cognition
Microsoft’s definition assumes human task coverage equals intelligence. It does not.

Absurdity 4 — No shared definition
SAFE AI Foundation confirms there is no consensus, making AGI a moving target.

Absurdity 5 — Academic definitions contradict industry
Academic AGI requires cognitive versatility, world‑model coherence, memory, reasoning, and transfer — none of which industry definitions guarantee.
